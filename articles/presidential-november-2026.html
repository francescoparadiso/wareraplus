Seven presidencies were decided on 1-3 November. Four changed party, five outgoing presidents did not stand, and three of the new presidents were serving ministers until the vote. The new governments are already taking shape: in the Netherlands the outgoing president is now vice president, in Italy the runner-up is back at Foreign Affairs.

The votes closed in the first minutes of 3 November. Across last month's six most populous nations plus , 1,632 people voted for a president, against 1,699 in October: a small fall (-3.9%) after October's big one. Only Sweden and the Netherlands gained voters. What changed a lot is who won. In October the common thread was a winner who stepped aside; in November it is the cabinet: in six of the seven races, one of the top two names had served as a minister in October's government (four of them were still in office on election day). Germany is the exception, where the winner was the sitting president.

Housekeeping first: active populations are down and the order has moved. fell from third to seventh, from 568 active citizens to 354, and has been overtaken by (368), a country that did not exist until 26 October and is now sixth in the game. That is why the Maldives get a special section after the seven: it voted for the first time, and its 312 votes would take the total to 1,944. Italy is 10th with 287 and stays in as usual.

The eight presidential results of 1-3 November in the WarEra+ Political View

One term used throughout: the effective number of candidates (ENP). It counts how many serious candidates a race really had: a ballot where one person takes 90% scores close to 1, a ballot split evenly between four scores 4. It says whether a country concentrated or fragmented since October, instead of guessing from the top two names.

A note on the data: populations and party membership are live numbers read between 09:00 and 09:45 UTC on 3 November, not snapshots of election day. Voters do not have to be party members, so "votes per member" below is a rough indicator, not a measure of loyalty. The governments were read live from the game in the same window, roughly nine hours after the polls closed. In the archive the cabinets were emptied by the vote and rebuilt by the presidents over the following hours, so they may still change, and three posts are vacant for now (listed as such). "Same as October" means the same person held the same post in the last October record; that comparison comes from the WarEra+ government archive, which samples every six hours. Backstage politics - who stepped aside, who made a deal with whom, why a party emptied - is not in the electoral data. What the seven countries have in common

1. The cabinet runs for the top. Three of the seven new presidents were ministers in the outgoing government: Defense in the Netherlands, Foreign Affairs in Egypt, Economy in Portugal. A fourth minister finished second: Foreign Affairs in Italy. Two more runners-up had left the cabinet in October: Sweden's, a former Minister of Economy, on 20 October, and Lithuania's, a former Minister of Economy and Vice President, by 23 October, who ran against the president they had served. Only Germany had no minister in the top two. In October the path to the presidency went through the party; in November it went through the cabinet.

2. The outgoing winner stepped aside, again. In five of the seven countries the October winner was not on the November ballot: Portugal, the Netherlands, Sweden, Egypt and Italy - the same count as last month. The two who ran, in Germany and Lithuania, both won a second term with a bigger share.

3. Party leaders win. In five countries the new president is the leader of their party: Portugal, the Netherlands, Sweden, Lithuania and Egypt. In Germany and Italy the winner is not the party leader.

4. Big parties, small votes. The party with the largest membership won only in Lithuania and Italy. Elsewhere the largest memberships polled almost nothing: 625 members and 16 votes in Portugal, 330 and 14 in Sweden, 266 and 15 in Germany, 227 and 35 in the Netherlands. And in Italy the party that won the last two presidencies has emptied to a single member. The Maldives, below, take the pattern to the extreme: the biggest party there polled 8 votes.

5. Presidency and congress, three out of seven. Comparing with the congresses of 7 October, the president's party is also the largest in congress in Portugal, the Netherlands and Lithuania. In Germany and Sweden it is second, in Egypt and Italy it is not even close.

6. Margins went up almost everywhere. The winning share rose in five countries: Portugal 52.4% → 75.3%, Lithuania 67.7% → 77.6%, the Netherlands 32.6% → 39.9%, Germany 43.3% → 46.8%, Egypt 50.7% → 53.0%. It fell in Sweden (70.1% → 68.0%) and, sharply, in Italy (75.0% → 56.3%), where the margin went from 78 votes to 15.

The new governments

32 of the 35 posts in the seven governments are filled. Portugal has no Minister of Economy, Lithuania no Minister of Foreign Affairs, Egypt no Vice President.

1. The vote empties the cabinet, and the presidents rebuild it. Of the 25 ministerial and vice-presidential posts filled so far, only 6 went to the person who held the same post in October: the Foreign Affairs ministers in Germany and Italy, the Economy ministers in Germany, the Netherlands, Lithuania and Egypt. Portugal's cabinet is entirely new.

2. Economy is the stickiest post. Four of the six Economy ministers in place - Germany, the Netherlands, Lithuania, Egypt - already held the job in October. Italy and Sweden named new ones, and Portugal's is vacant.

3. Outgoing presidents stay close. Of the five who stood aside, two are in the new cabinet: as Vice President in the Netherlands, as Minister of Defense in Italy. In Portugal, Sweden and Egypt they are not.

4. The beaten side gets a seat. In Italy the runner-up is again Minister of Foreign Affairs. In Sweden the October runner-up, who did not stand, is Foreign Minister. In Portugal the Minister of Defense leads the party that held the presidency until October.
